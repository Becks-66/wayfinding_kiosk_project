Finding Your Way:
A concept for smarter campus navigation

Case study inspired by the navigation problems inside the psychology faculty at the University of Padova (UNIPD).

01

The problem

At the psychology faculty of UNIPD, navigation problems arise mainly from the architecture itself. The five buildings of the complex are nearly identical, their names are barely visible from the outside (small, easy-to-miss signs), and internal navigation is difficult because there is no visual differentiation between floors and no clear signage. Moreover, the disposition of some rooms, break mental models. You look for the library expecting a building, but it sits on the fifth floors of another one; the labs are scattered across a single building, some in the basement, some tucked next to professors' offices. Rooms for lessons, are scattered as well. New students, Erasmus students, and participants in experiments often get lost or arrive late. Personally, I studied there and I got lost there, plenty of times.
02

My approach

A full signage overhaul would be needed, but that would mean new markings, a new colour logic across five occupied buildings, months of closures for students and professors.

And even then, a perfect sign has a limit: it can only carry a handful of fixed destinations. But a department holds hundreds of rooms, labs, and offices. This is where a digital kiosk fits: you arrive at the complex, you search your destination, you get a path to follow. The already existing signs, even if few, can accompany you along the way.

The university already offers on its website an online virtual tour with 3D buildings for the faculty of psychology, so I decided to start researching from there before designing.

03

What already exists

The virtual tour presented some critical points:

•The tour begins from a position where no entrance actually exists, while the three real accesses to the complex aren't represented at all. The tour isn’t usable as a quick tool for orientation: you have to already know the place to make sense of it.
•Clicking on a building catapults you onto a floor chosen by the site (usually the lowest floor), and only then can you decide where to go. If you're looking for a specific room and don't know where it is, you have to dig through nested menus and maps. There is no way to search for a destination directly.
https://virtualtour.dpg.psy.unipd.it/
•Once inside a floor, the situation is messy. The map shows a big block of colored boxes. The colors signal different department labs (animal psychology, psychophysiology, ...), but there is no clear legend, and the colors aren't unique: they're reused elsewhere in the overall map. A new visitor can’t easily decode it.
•The floor view doesn't start from where you actually enter it (a door or the stairs), but from an arbitrarily chosen position. A small compass indicates north, which helps the few who navigate by cardinal directions, but a visitor who simply wants to know “where do I go from here?” gets no answer from a compass.

The tour gave me, in negative, the brief for the kiosk: everything that doesn’t work there, is what the new interface needs to do well.

04

My interface

The kiosk works in three steps, and each one answers a failure of the virtual tour.

1. ARRIVE
The screen shows a 2D bird's-eye view of the complex, with a “you are here” pin at the real entrance where the kiosk stands. A hint invites the visitor to search their destination. There will be one kiosk at each of the three possible entrances to the complex.
2. SEARCH
The visitor can search directly the room, lab, office or service that’s needed. There are no nested menus, and no need to already know where things are.
3. ROUTE
The screen shows the route for the searched destination step by step. Since the buildings have multiple floors, the map is segmented: one screen shows how to reach the right building, one how to reach the stairs and one the final path to your destination. At the end, a QR code lets you take the path with you, downloading the a static maps on your phone.
05

Design choices

A segmented route

One long path across five buildings and multiple floors is hard to hold in memory, but three short segments, each with one goal, are not. The segmentation also mirrors the logic of physical signage: first the building, the floor, then the room.

Color choice

In the complex, buildings haven’t a single specific function: the library lives inside Psico2, offices and rooms for lessons are everywhere): for this reason, coloring by function wasn’t possible. For the kiosk, each of the five buildings has one stable color identity, so when you search a destination, the whole journey takes the color of the building in it. For each building, I selected three shades of the assigned color: a pastel shade for the resting state, a darker shade for the stroke of the building, and a vibrant one to highlight the path to follow and the room to reach.

Why a 2D?

Maps can be represented both in 2D with a bird eye-view, or in 3D. Both approaches ask different things of the user: 3D offers a realistic first-person view, while 2D works through symbolic conventions people have practiced on a lifetime of city plans and Google Maps. Representing the space with a 3D map, would’ve been reasonable: research on mobile indoor navigation has found real advantages for 3D views followed in real time while walking (Burigat et al., 2017). However, my kiosk follows a different model: you consult it before moving, memorize your path, and the signage accompanies you inside multi-levels buildings. For this “look-then-go” interaction, the evidence favors 2D: in multi-level buildings, top-down maps produced more accurate cross-floor understanding and were preferred by most users (Li & Giudice, 2013). Moreover, a kiosk is fixed: it is the "you are here" point. So, the main strength of 3D (matching the screen map to the surrounding) is already solved. Finally, a 2D map is simpler and cheaper to build and maintain, which for a university is not a detail.
•Burigat, S., Chittaro, L., & Sioni, R. (2017). Mobile three-dimensional maps for wayfinding in large and complex buildings: Empirical comparison of first-person versus third-person perspective.
•Li, H., & Giudice, N. A. (2013, November). The effects of 2D and 3D maps on learning virtual multi-level indoor environments.

The prototype

Below, an interactive prototype of the kiosk, designed first in Figma, then built with Claude Design. The maps are redrawn from scratch, and kept deliberately minimal. For the scope of the prototype, only the route for Biblioteca Metelli, Laboratorio E08 and Aula T3 are available.

07

Limitations

This is a speculative project built without client input or access to institutional resources.

•Source material: no up-to-date floor plans were available. The maps were traced from two sources: the department's 3D virtual tour, viewed from above (which does not produce a true bird's-eye projection and is therefore slightly distorted), and emergency evacuation plans over five years old.
•Scope and validation: the prototype covers four hard-coded routes. There is no routing engine or content backend, so the system's behaviour at full building scale remains untested. No usability testing was carried out, so the design decisions are only grounded in environmental psychology research and spatial reasoning.
•Queuing issue: animated transitions and multi-step interactions slow down each session. During peak periods, like the start of semester or exam months, queuing is likely.
•Accessibility: the current design is not optimised for blind and low-vision users.
•Languages: only Italian and English are available at the moment. Adding further languages would require data on the actual composition of the international student population at the department, which was not available.
